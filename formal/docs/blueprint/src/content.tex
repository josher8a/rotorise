\section{§1 The attribute domain}\label{1-the-attribute-domain}

A store holds items under names, and every value it holds, whatever type a schema gives it, is
drawn from one shared domain of attributes. This section fixes that domain.

Everything a table holds lives in one set. Types are read as retracts of it, which is the device
this whole specification rests on.

\begin{verbatim}
-- members, and the attribute domain over them
m  ∈ Member  ::= S | N | B | BOOL | NULL | SS | NS | BS | L | M
a  ∈ 𝔸       ::= ⟨m, v⟩        where v ∈ Carries(m)

-- an item is an M without its wrapper: the one place a map is not written as one
i  ∈ Item    ::= Name ⇀fin 𝔸
\end{verbatim}

The coproduct is \textbf{exact}: an attribute carries one member and no other. That is a fact about the
wire format and it is load-bearing here — \texttt{⟨S,\ "1"⟩} and \texttt{⟨N,\ "1"⟩} are distinct elements of \texttt{𝔸}, so
a codec choosing between them is choosing between distinguishable things.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Member & \texttt{Carries(m)} & \\
\midrule
\texttt{S} & \texttt{String} & not normalised \\
\texttt{N} & \texttt{Numerals} & optional sign, digits, optional point, optional exponent; ≤ 38 significant digits; magnitude 0 or \texttt{1e-130\ …\ 9.9…e125} \\
\texttt{B} & \texttt{Bytes} & \\
\texttt{BOOL} & \texttt{Bool} & \\
\texttt{NULL} & \texttt{𝟙} & present, and carrying nothing \\
\texttt{SS} & \texttt{℘⁺fin(String)} & non-empty; unordered \\
\texttt{NS} & \texttt{℘⁺fin(Numerals)} & non-empty; unordered \\
\texttt{BS} & \texttt{℘⁺fin(Bytes)} & non-empty; unordered \\
\texttt{L} & \texttt{𝔸*} & ordered; heterogeneous \\
\texttt{M} & \texttt{Name\ ⇀fin\ 𝔸} & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\texttt{Numerals} admits an exponent — DynamoDB states its own range \cite{dynamodb-types} as \texttt{1E-130} to \texttt{9.9999…E+125}, so
\texttt{"1e+21"} is a well-formed \texttt{N} and not a defect. The bound that bites is \emph{magnitude}, not notation.

Note where \texttt{℘} appears and where it does not. \texttt{SS} is a \emph{set} and \texttt{L} is a \emph{sequence}; nothing in the
domain identifies them. Every claim later about telling a set from an array traces back to this line.

\section{§2 What a codec is}\label{2-what-a-codec-is}

A partial retraction of the attribute domain onto a set of values, together with the equivalence its
own reading induces.

\begin{verbatim}
c = (T, μ, e, p)      T ⊆ Value     μ ∈ Member ∪ {⊤}
                      e : T ⇀ 𝔸    encode — may fail
                      p : 𝔸 ⇀ T    decode — may fail
\end{verbatim}

\begin{verbatim}
v ∈ Value ::= str(String) | num(WFNumeral) | bool(Bool) | bin(Bytes) | null
            | strSet(℘⁺fin(String)) | numSet(℘⁺fin(WFNumeral)) | binSet(℘⁺fin(Bytes))
            | arr(Value*) | map(Name ⇀fin Value)

k ∈ Kind  ::= string | number | boolean | binary | set | array | map | null
\end{verbatim}

\texttt{T} is a set of values, the language of things a codec holds. Ten constructors, and eight
kinds: the three set constructors are all of kind \texttt{set}, and every other constructor is a kind
of its own. \texttt{WFNumeral} is §1's well-formed numerals. \texttt{Value} is not §1's \texttt{Member}, but
the two carry a number the same way: an \texttt{N} carries a \texttt{WFNumeral}, and \texttt{num} carries the same
\texttt{WFNumeral}, exactly. A host whose numeric type is not exact adapts that type at the port. \texttt{μ\ =\ ⊤} reads as
\emph{not determined by the term}, not as \emph{anything}. Which terms may leave it
undetermined is settled in §3.

The record and its laws take the carriers as parameters: the values, the attributes, and the
members. No law reads the structure of a value or of an attribute, and \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} reads only the
member of an attribute. This document states the calculus at the DynamoDB carriers: \texttt{Value},
§1's \texttt{𝔸} and \texttt{Member}. The development states the same laws, the term language and
soundness at a JSON carrier too (\texttt{Calculus.JSON}).

\subsection{Laws}\label{laws}

\begin{definition}[(R) retraction]
\label{cond:R}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.Lawful.retract}
\leanok
\texttt{∀\ t\ ∈\ dom(e).\ p(e(t))\ =\ t}. Writing then reading returns the value written.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[(C) canonical reads]
\label{cond:C}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.Lawful.canon}
\leanok
\texttt{∀\ a\ ∈\ dom(p).\ e(p(a))\ =\ a}. Whatever was read is written back, at the attribute it was
read from.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[(W) a term writes its member]
\label{cond:W}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.Lawful.writes}
\leanok
\texttt{μ\ ≠\ ⊤\ ⟹\ ∀\ t\ ∈\ dom(e).\ e(t)\ =\ ⟨μ,\ \_⟩}. A term that names its member writes that member
and no other.
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}[(MP) member preservation]
\label{cond:MP}
\uses{cond:C}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.Lawful.canon}
\leanok
\texttt{∀\ a\ ∈\ dom(p).\ member(e(p(a)))\ =\ member(a)}. Reading and writing back never changes a stored
member. It is immediate from \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}, because \texttt{e(p(a))} is \texttt{a}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[the three laws are independent]
\label{thm:laws-independent}
\uses{cond:R,cond:C,cond:W}
\lean{Calculus.cR_notR, Calculus.cR_canon, Calculus.cR_W, Calculus.cC_R, Calculus.cC_notCanon, Calculus.cC_W, Calculus.cW_notW, Calculus.cW_R, Calculus.cW_canon}
\leanok
No one of \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}, \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} and \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} follows from the other two: for each
there is a pair satisfying the other two and failing that one.
\end{theorem}

A weaker form of \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} asks only that a read value be writable:
\texttt{∀\ a\ ∈\ dom(p).\ p(a)\ ∈\ dom(e)}. It does not imply \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}: \texttt{cM} reads \texttt{⟨BOOL,\ true⟩}
to a string, and writes that string at \texttt{S} (\texttt{cM\_core}, \texttt{cM\_notCanon}). Every term whose obligations hold
satisfies \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}. A read extension \texttt{extendRead(c,\ r)} is not a term. It is an outer carrier that
preserves \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}, \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} and the weaker form under its two premises
(\texttt{LawfulCore}), while its alternate reader can break \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}.

\rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} and \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} are both equalities on the nose: \texttt{p\ ∘\ e\ =\ id} on
\texttt{dom(e)} and \texttt{e\ ∘\ p\ =\ id} on \texttt{dom(p)}. So a lawful pair is a partial bijection. For a pair
outside the laws \texttt{e\ ∘\ p\ =\ id} can fail: an alternate reader reads an attribute that the current
encoding writes differently, and \texttt{e\_comp\_p\_ne\_id} is that witness. In the language of quotient
lenses \cite{quotient-lenses} \texttt{e\ ∘\ p} is then a \textbf{canonizer}, total on \texttt{dom(p)} by the
weaker form, and the equivalence it works modulo is not a parameter a programmer chooses: it is
\texttt{ker\ p}, forced by the reading itself. Write \texttt{a\ ≈c\ a′\ ⟺\ p(a)\ =\ p(a′)}.

\subsection{Stability under a generator}\label{corollary-q--stability}

\begin{corollary}[Q — stability]
\label{cor:Q}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.Lawful.stable}
\leanok
\texttt{∀\ a\ ∈\ dom(p).\ e(p(a))\ ≈c\ a}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
\uses{cond:C,cond:R}
\leanok
By \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}, \texttt{p(a)\ ∈\ dom(e)}. Put \texttt{t\ =\ p(a)}. By \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}, \texttt{p(e(t))\ =\ t}. Substituting,
\texttt{p(e(p(a)))\ =\ p(a)}, which is \texttt{e(p(a))\ ≈c\ a} by definition of \texttt{≈c}.
\end{proof}

So stability is retraction asked at a different \emph{source of values}: \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} at values a generator
produced, \rulelink{cor:Q}{\textbf{(Q)}} at values a table returned. What \rulelink{cor:Q}{\textbf{(Q)}} adds to
\rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} is exactly \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}, and that containment is the thing most likely to be untrue.

\subsection{What the readable side owes}\label{corollary-read-side-w}

\begin{corollary}[read-side W]
\label{cor:readW}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.Lawful.reads}
\leanok
\texttt{μ\ ≠\ ⊤\ ⟹\ ∀\ a\ ∈\ dom(p).\ member(a)\ =\ μ}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
\uses{cond:C,cond:W}
\leanok
\texttt{e(p(a))\ =\ a} by \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}, so \texttt{a\ =\ ⟨μ,\ \_⟩} by \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}}.
\end{proof}

\rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} constrains only the writing half; this corollary is what makes the reading half follow. A
\texttt{⇝\ S} term does not merely write \texttt{S}: it \emph{accepts} nothing else, or a read-modify-write could
smuggle a member change through. The outer read extension \texttt{extendRead} is the sanctioned violation: it reads members the
current encoding no longer writes, and re-encoding them is the migration.

\rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} binds hardest where \texttt{μ\ =\ ⊤}. A term with a \texttt{μ} is pinned by \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} and the corollary above;
a term without one chooses per value, and \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} and \rulelink{cor:Q}{\textbf{(Q)}} do not stop it choosing differently on
the way back. A codec reading \texttt{⟨N,\ "1.5"⟩} to a string and writing \texttt{⟨S,\ "1.5"⟩} satisfies both — the
\emph{value} survives — while silently changing the stored type, so a numeric sort key stops ordering and a
typed index rejects the write. It holds per attribute and recursively: a member change three levels
inside an \texttt{M} is the same failure.

This is why \texttt{opaque} reads \texttt{N} as a numeral and never as a string. It writes the numeral
back at \texttt{N}, so the member survives. That is \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} at \texttt{opaque}, and §2 without it could not say
so.

Write \texttt{Can(c)\ =\ e{[}T{]}} for the \textbf{canonical} attributes: those a codec will actually produce. By
\rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} a lawful codec reads exactly \texttt{Can(c)}. It is total on \texttt{Can(c)} and merely partial on
\texttt{𝔸}, which is the precise sense in which a table is more permissive than the schema describing
it.

\subsection{What a codec ideally is}\label{what-a-codec-ideally-is}

Those pieces assemble into something simpler than the three laws suggest, and that is where the
complexity lives.

\textbf{Between \texttt{dom(e)} and \texttt{Can(c)}, a codec is a bijection.} \texttt{p\ ∘\ e\ =\ id} there is \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}; \texttt{e\ ∘\ p\ =\ id}
there is \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}; and \texttt{e} is injective by \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}. No quotient, no choice. On that part a
codec is a plain isomorphism between a set of values and a set of attributes, and there is nothing
further to say about it.

A bijection extends in \textbf{two directions}, and a lawful codec takes one of them:

\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
& extension & what it buys & where it lives \\
\midrule
\textbf{quotient} & \texttt{dom(p)\ ⊇\ Can(c)}, strictly & reading back what an older encoding wrote, not only what you wrote & the alternate reader, outside the laws: \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} refuses it \\
\textbf{partiality} & \texttt{dom(e)\ ⊆\ T}, \texttt{dom(p)\ ⊊\ 𝔸} & refusing values and attributes that are not yours & every lawful codec \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

So \texttt{e\ ∘\ p\ =\ id} holds for every lawful codec, and fails only for the alternate reader, which
extends the reading past \texttt{Can(c)}. There the canonizer maps each class to its representative. The
literature has a name for each extension: quotient lenses \cite{quotient-lenses} for the first, and
partial isomorphisms \cite{invertible-syntax} for the second. This calculus needs both, the
first outside its terms, which is why neither reference alone is the right one. The quotient
reading refines the asymmetric lens of \cite{tree-transformations}, and \cite{lens-laws} is where the
variants are told apart.

The same two relaxations reappear one level up. §3's \texttt{held} is an iso and
\texttt{refine} its prism. The outer read extension \texttt{extendRead(c,\ r)} satisfies
\texttt{extendRead(c,\ r)\ ≈\ c} on \texttt{Can(c)}. That is the quotient again, outside the term
algebra.

\subsection{When the deviations vanish}\label{when-the-deviations-vanish}

Both extensions can be driven to zero, and asking what it takes is the cleanest way to see what
each one is really for. They do not have the same answer, and one term alone answers both at
once.

\textbf{The quotient collapses} when \texttt{ker\ p} (\texttt{Codec.Ker}) is equality — when no two distinct attributes
read to one value. \textbf{Every lawful codec collapses it.} By \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} an attribute that reads to
\texttt{t} is \texttt{e(t)}, so two attributes that read to one value are one attribute. So every term
whose obligations hold collapses it. A held-free term collapses it with no law as a premise: \texttt{confluence\_R} puts its
reading inside \texttt{opaque}'s, and \texttt{Ω\_canon} makes \texttt{opaque}'s reading injective. Outside the
term algebra, \texttt{extendRead} keeps a quotient, and \texttt{e\_comp\_p\_ne\_id} is its witness.

\begin{theorem}[a lawful codec has \texttt{ker\ p} equality]
\label{thm:ker-heldfree}
\lean{Calculus.Lawful.ker_eq, Calculus.ker_eq_of_HF}
\leanok
At a lawful codec, two attributes that read to one value are equal. At a held-free term the same
holds with no law as a premise.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{The store is not a quotient of the codec.} \texttt{η}, the store's own normalisation of a stored
numeral (§3), rewrites \texttt{N}. This is the medium, and it is the calculus's one concession to something it
does not model. A codec survives the store when \texttt{η} lies inside \texttt{ker\ p} (\texttt{SurvivesStore}), and that
buys \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} through the store (\texttt{Lawful.retract\_through\_store}). With \texttt{ker\ p} equality, the inclusion
asks \texttt{η} to fix every canonical attribute. \texttt{number} writes \texttt{1.500}, which the store rewrites, so \texttt{number} does not
survive (\texttt{not\_survivesStore\_numberCodec}). The DynamoDB port's \texttt{decimalCodec} is gated at \texttt{Stored}, the
numerals \texttt{η} leaves alone. Its \texttt{ker\ p} is equality as well (\texttt{ker\_decimalCodec}), and it survives
(\texttt{survivesStore\_decimalCodec}). The gate absorbs the store, and no kernel does.

\begin{theorem}[a lawful codec survives the store only at a fixed point]
\label{thm:store-heldfree}
\lean{Calculus.Lawful.survivesStore_fix}
\leanok
A lawful codec survives the store only where \texttt{η} fixes each canonical attribute.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Partiality collapses} when \texttt{dom(p)\ =\ 𝔸} — when every attribute the domain admits is one this
codec reads. And here the answer is structural rather than a matter of care: \textbf{§1 fixes one \texttt{𝔸}
for many \texttt{T}.} A table holds items of several schemas; a codec is a retract of the domain, not a
description of it. So partiality is not a tax on textual representation, and no choice of format
removes it. It is the price of the domain being \emph{shared}, and it would be paid identically by a
free algebra, a fixed-width binary layout, or a memory image, as long as more than one \texttt{T} lives in
one \texttt{𝔸}. What removes it is one schema per store, which is a different architecture rather than a
better encoding.

\textbf{They collapse together at \texttt{opaque} alone.} \texttt{opaque} reads every attribute of \texttt{𝔸}
(\texttt{opaqA\_total}). Its pair is a bijection between \texttt{Value} and \texttt{𝔸} (\texttt{Ω\_onto}, \texttt{Ω\_canon}), so its
\texttt{ker\ p} is equality. Its held set is every \texttt{Value}, so one \texttt{T} lives in \texttt{𝔸}, which is the
condition the paragraph above names. \texttt{opaque} pays with \texttt{μ\ =\ ⊤}. Every term with a schema keeps one deviation at least:
every term whose obligations hold kills the quotient and keeps partiality, and §1 is why.

One deviation \emph{does} have a clean term-level answer, and it is often mistaken for the other
two. Ambiguity in a \texttt{union} is not a property of the medium at all. It is what happens when
two options disagree where they overlap. A union of compatible options has no ambiguity to resolve,
and \rulelink{rule:dunion}{\texttt{(dunion)}} is the tagged case: the tag reaches one variant by construction.
Whatever the storage looks like, that collapse is available by declaring the options compatible,
or by declaring the discriminator.

\section{§3 The judgement}\label{3-the-judgement}

Two indices, because a codec has two sides: the values it holds, and the member it stores them in.
Both are needed, and neither determines the other.

\begin{verbatim}
⊢ c : T ⇝ μ        c holds T, and stores it as μ
\end{verbatim}

\texttt{number} holds a \texttt{WFNumeral}: §1's numeral grammar with its bound, at most 38 significant
digits and magnitude zero or \texttt{1e-130\ …\ 9.9…e125}. \texttt{number} is \textbf{not} restricted to integers,
and not to \texttt{2⁵³}: \texttt{2⁵³\ +\ 1} is a numeral of its own. The bound is the type of the carrier, so
\texttt{e} and \texttt{p} are constructor wrappers with no gate, and \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} is the identity on the
numeral. The calculus axiomatizes no numeric type. A host whose number is a binary64 float adapts the
float to a \texttt{WFNumeral} with \rulelink{rule:held}{\texttt{(held)}}, and that rule states the obligation.

A term that writes an attribute outside §1's \texttt{𝔸} is not a term of this calculus. So the bound is the
type of the \texttt{N} carrier, and not a precondition recorded and left to the service.

\rulelink{rule:literal}{\texttt{(literal)}} gates at the \textbf{declaration}, alongside \texttt{n\ ≥\ 1} and pairwise
distinctness, because \texttt{ℓ} is data the term owns and a premise on data is decidable when the codec is
built. The premise bounds the writable side: \texttt{ℓ} is fixed when the codec is built, so every value
\texttt{e} will ever accept has been checked already. The readable side needs no gate: an \texttt{N} attribute
carries a \texttt{WFNumeral}, so a numeric \texttt{literal} reads an attribute exactly where its numeral is one of
\texttt{ℓ}.

A subset of the numerals chosen by the caller — a finite set of admitted numbers — is
\texttt{refine(number,\ T)}, and has no rule of its own because it needs none. A term whose \texttt{p} returned the
numeral it read \emph{without} verifying membership in \texttt{T} would break \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}, and with it the
claim that \texttt{:\ T} says anything; a term that does verify is \texttt{refine(number,\ T)} on all four fields. An
unchecked narrowing of a read is a cast, and a cast belongs to \texttt{refine}, which does the check, or to
\texttt{held}, which is where the caller's own functions live.

\texttt{decimal} holds a numeral as text. It is \rulelink{rule:held}{\texttt{(held)}} over \texttt{number} at the text adapter
\texttt{numeralOfStoredText}/\texttt{textOfNumeral}, so it holds text and stores \texttt{N}.

Its held set is \texttt{Stored}, and not §1's \texttt{Numerals}, because those are \textbf{different sets}. \texttt{Numerals}
is what the store \emph{accepts}; \texttt{Stored} is what it \emph{hands back}. DynamoDB normalises every \texttt{N} it
takes — \texttt{1.500} becomes \texttt{1.5}, \texttt{007} becomes \texttt{7}, \texttt{1e5} becomes \texttt{100000}, \texttt{-0} becomes \texttt{0} — and
\texttt{decimal}'s \texttt{p} is the identity on the digits, so anything outside the normal form is written as one
string and read as another.

This is the calculus's one concession to something it does not model. §2 composes \texttt{e} and \texttt{p}
directly; in use there is a store between them, and it is not the identity on attributes. Writing
\texttt{η} for the store's normalisation, a codec survives the round trip through it exactly when \textbf{\texttt{η}
lies inside \texttt{ker\ p}} — \texttt{≈c} of §2, the relation that already exists for "a different attribute
reading to the same value". \texttt{number} does not satisfy it: \texttt{p} is the identity on the numeral, so
\texttt{p(1.500)} and \texttt{p(1.5)} are two values. \texttt{decimal}'s \texttt{ker\ p} is equality as well. So the held set is narrowed to the
fixed points of \texttt{η}, which is what \texttt{Stored} is. \texttt{η} is observed, not stipulated: it is the store's
behaviour, and the calculus can only be arranged around it.

\subsection{A ground term either takes its member whole, or it is a refinement}\label{a-ground-term-either-takes-its-member-whole-or-it-is-a-refinement}

Read the ground terms together and they fall into two kinds, with nothing in between.
\texttt{string}, \texttt{boolean}, \texttt{binary}, \texttt{null} and \texttt{number} take their member's carrier
\textbf{whole} — every string is a \texttt{String}, every numeral a \texttt{WFNumeral} — and there is nothing for
them to check. \texttt{literal(ℓ)} wants a \emph{subset} of what the store will hold at its member, and is
\texttt{refine} over the ground term \texttt{g} at the predicate \texttt{∈\ ℓ}. \texttt{decimal} is not a ground term:
it is \rulelink{rule:held}{\texttt{(held)}} over \texttt{number}.

This is not a resemblance. A predicate wanted on \textbf{both} halves \emph{is} \texttt{refine}, because \texttt{e} and \texttt{p}
are partial and refusing is what the error channel does. So a gate is never a rule's private
business, and its predicate belongs in the term's admissible set, which is why a literal's held
values are \texttt{ℓ} (§5): gating both ways is a term deciding its own membership.

\textbf{Containers are the other kind, and they are not refinements.} \texttt{set}, \texttt{array}, \texttt{tuple}, \texttt{record}
and \texttt{object} narrow by their \emph{subterms} and their \emph{names}, never by a predicate on the whole value,
so there is no \texttt{q} for an admissible set to carry. §5 records the same fact from the other side: a
list, a record and an object admit their empty case whatever their element admits, so no set of one
container's own values separates it from another. A set has no empty case, because \texttt{℘⁺fin} has no
empty inhabitant. Scalars are refined by predicates; containers are refined structurally, and only the first kind
has a gate to record.

\texttt{literal} is the one ground term whose member follows from its \emph{values} rather than from its name,
and the rule says so by reading both off a base: \texttt{ℓ} must lie in some ground term's held set, and
what that term stores is what the literal stores. One rule covers \texttt{S} and \texttt{N}, and covers \texttt{BOOL},
\texttt{B} and \texttt{NULL} with nothing added — a boolean literal is \texttt{literal(true)}, at base \texttt{boolean}. The
base is an index the derivation carries and the term does not write. An alternative's kind determines
it. A host's \texttt{decimal} literal is a \texttt{number} literal under the text adapter, so text wanted stored as
\texttt{N} needs no base of its own. Its held side is the string literals of its texts.

The rule is also not doing anything \texttt{refine} does not, which is why \texttt{literal}
reads as the abbreviation it is. \texttt{literal(ℓ)} is \texttt{refine(g,\ ∈\ ℓ)}, and the general fact behind every
literal union is one statement rather than a rule per member:

\begin{quote}
For \textbf{any derivable base}, a union of refinements with pairwise disjoint predicates admits
uniquely on both sides.
\end{quote}

\texttt{literal} at \texttt{string} and at \texttt{number} are two instantiations; \texttt{boolean} and \texttt{binary} are two more;
and the base need not be ground at all — two disjoint refinements of one \texttt{array(string)} separate by
the same argument. \emph{One base with disjoint predicates} is what the rule turns on, not the
literal.
What \texttt{literal} adds over writing \texttt{refine} is that \texttt{ℓ} is data the term owns, so both admissible
sets carry it (§5) and a reader sees the enumeration.

There is deliberately no derivation for a mixed set: a codec writes one member, and one \texttt{g} cannot
hold both a string and a numeral, so \texttt{literal(ℓ,\ k)} with \texttt{ℓ\ ∈\ String} and \texttt{k\ ∈\ WFNumeral} has none. That is \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} and not an omission, and it is not
a limitation either — the spelling is \texttt{union(literal(ℓ),\ literal(k))}, whose options separate on
both sides for free, by distinct members on the readable one and disjoint kinds on the writable.
What the union concludes is \texttt{⊤}, which is the honest index for a term that writes two members. A union of
literals all at the number base is what gives the term for an enum indexed by number — the held set is the literal union and the
stored member is \texttt{N}, checked in both directions — and \texttt{⇝\ N} carries on the wire the same \texttt{WFNumeral}
that the held set holds, so \rulelink{cond:R}{\texttt{(R)}} is the identity on the numeral.

A variable is a de Bruijn index: \texttt{var\ 0} in \texttt{b} stands for \texttt{mu\ b} itself. A closed term reads a variable

variable under a list or an object. It never reads the variable at the binder's own input or under a \texttt{held}.
The guard refuses \texttt{mu(var\ 0)} (\texttt{not\_typed\_unguarded\_binder}) and \texttt{mu(union(var\ 0,\ string))}
(\texttt{guard\_refuses\_option\_at\_binder\_input}). It also refuses a \texttt{held} between the binder and its variable
(\texttt{guard\_refuses\_held\_on\_path}), because the conversion of a \texttt{held} can make the input deeper.

\begin{theorem}[guarded recursion]
\label{thm:guarded-recursion}
\uses{rule:mu,cond:R,cond:C,cond:W}
\lean{Calculus.muCodec, Calculus.Alg.contractive_body, Calculus.muStage_agree, Calculus.muCodec_e_fix, Calculus.muCodec_p_fix, Calculus.lawful_muCodec}
\leanok
\texttt{mu\ b} denotes the limit of the stages of its body: the stages start at the bottom, and each input reads
the stage its own depth names, on the write side by the depth of the value and on the read side by the
depth of the attribute. The guard makes the body contractive: it reads its argument only at inputs strictly
shallower than its own input, at least one layer down. From that alone the stages agree past the depth of the input. The limit is a fixed point of
the body on both sides, and lawful stages give a lawful limit. No two options of a body need be disjoint.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[(opaque)]
\label{rule:opaque}
\lean{Calculus.Typed.opaq}
\leanok
\begin{verbatim}
─────────────────────────── (opaque)
⊢ opaque : Value ⇝ ⊤
\end{verbatim}
\end{definition}

These \texttt{⊤}s are forced for different reasons, and the distinction matters. \texttt{mu} concludes \texttt{⊤}, because its
stages need not agree on one member. \texttt{held} carries a pair of opaque maps and there is nothing to ask, which is also why its
premise is the cancellation law itself: nothing structural guarantees it, so the rule demands it.
Demanding \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} alone would under-demand, deriving a \texttt{⊤} term free to change stored members — the
exact term §2's argument for \texttt{opaque} says cannot exist. (\rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} is vacuous at \texttt{⊤}, so the three
named are the whole obligation.)

\texttt{opaque} is the calculus's universal sum, and it is primitive only in presentation: it
is \rulelink{thm:guarded-recursion}{guarded recursion} at one fixed body. The body is the \textbf{union of everything
self-identifying, closed under the containers}, a fixpoint of shape
\texttt{union(string,\ boolean,\ binary,\ ⟨NULL⟩,\ number,\ set(opaque),\ array(opaque),\ record(opaque))}
(\texttt{opaqStep}, \texttt{contractive\_opaqStep}). Its options are \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}}, because their kinds and
members are pairwise disjoint (\texttt{compat\_opaque}). That makes each layer lawful, and the limit is lawful by the
general theorem (\texttt{lawful\_opaq}). The held set is \emph{defined}
as the values whose member is recoverable from the value alone, so \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} holds by construction.
A numeral is such a value: the \texttt{number} option is \texttt{numberCodec}, which holds the numeral exactly, as its digits, and
\texttt{number\ ⇄\ N} keeps the member and the digits on both halves. So the held set is every
\texttt{Value} (\texttt{opaq\_total}).

\textbf{\texttt{opaque} has no numeric policy.} A host whose numeric type is not exact, a binary64 float for
example, adapts that type outside the core. A policy that refuses a host number, or one that rounds it,
is \rulelink{rule:held}{\texttt{(held)}} over \texttt{opaque}, and that rule states its obligation.

\texttt{held} and \texttt{refine} are the same shape at different strengths: an \textbf{iso} and a \textbf{prism}. The
literature collapses them — invertible syntax descriptions \cite{invertible-syntax} builds everything from an algebra of
partial isomorphisms, which is the merged combinator.

They are one term, in the following sense. Write the merged combinator
as an adapter that may refuse on either half — \texttt{f} in front of the writing, \texttt{g} behind the reading:

\begin{verbatim}
e = λv. f(v) ⨾ e_c        p = λa. p_c(a) ⨾ g
\end{verbatim}

\texttt{held(c,\ f,\ g)} is that with both halves \textbf{total}, and \texttt{refine(c,\ q)} is that with a \textbf{partial
identity} on both halves — refuse off \texttt{q}, pass through on it. The pairs agree on the nose, not up
to anything. So \texttt{refine} is not a second kind of adapter; it is the one adapter with a weaker
premise, and its whole content is that refusing \emph{is} available, because \texttt{e} and \texttt{p} are partial to
begin with.

They are kept apart for a reason outside the calculus, and only that one: a total \texttt{g} lets a
generator for \texttt{I} be pushed forward to one for \texttt{T}, while a partial one leaves only rejection
sampling. Same term, different search. The split is a note about testing, written into the syntax
so it cannot be lost, which is the same bargain \texttt{nullable} strikes with \texttt{union(c,\ null)}, and
should be read the same way.

\texttt{extendRead}'s \texttt{r} is a bare partial map and not a codec, because only one direction is ever used: the
rule writes through \texttt{c} whatever it read through. Asking for a codec would ask for a half that cannot
be called, and would \emph{force} one wherever the older encoding's held set differs. The two side
premises are the soundness proof's levies (§5). \texttt{ran(r)\ ⊆\ dom(e)} is \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}'s: a migration must
land on values the current encoding can write, or the read succeeds and the write-back is the
failure. The \textbf{coherence} premise — \texttt{r} agrees with \texttt{p} wherever both are defined — is what makes
\texttt{extendRead} a migration and not a disguise. Without it the adapter gives two conflicting
answers to one attribute. With it, \texttt{p\ ∪\ r} is a left-biased union of compatible partial maps,
and a migrated row behaves \emph{by law} like a current one at the surface. How the migration achieves
that is the migration's duty, but that it must is the calculus's.

\texttt{nullable} carries \textbf{no side premise}. The
danger is real — a subterm moving \texttt{null} onto another member, or another member onto \texttt{null}, would
let the wrapper's \texttt{NULL} branch shadow a real value, failing \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} in one direction and \rulelink{cond:MP}{\textbf{(MP)}}
in the other, and the fix is not a premise but the wrapper's own definition. \texttt{nullable(c)} adds the
null point \textbf{unless \texttt{c} already owns it}, asked of both halves at once:

\begin{verbatim}
owns(c)  ⟺  null ∈ dom(e_c)  ∨  ⟨NULL, ∗⟩ ∈ dom(p_c)
\end{verbatim}

Where \texttt{owns(c)}, the pair is \texttt{c}'s untouched. Where it does not, \texttt{c} reaches neither end of the null
point, and then \textbf{null-honesty is a theorem}: if \texttt{e\_c} wrote
\texttt{⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩}, \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} would make \texttt{p\_c} read it straight back, and \texttt{owns(c)} would hold; if \texttt{p\_c} read
some attribute to \texttt{null}, \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} would make \texttt{e\_c} write the point, and \texttt{owns(c)} would hold again.
Both hypotheses are refuted, not required.

The guard must be \textbf{joint}. Asked per side, \texttt{c} could decline to write \texttt{null} while still reading
\texttt{⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩} — the null point's two ends would belong to different options, the round trip would go
out through one and back through the other, and \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} would break with nothing looking ambiguous
on either side taken alone.

None of this is a new kind of condition. \texttt{nullable(c)} is structurally the two-option union
\texttt{union(c,\ ⟨NULL⟩-point)}, and its honesty conditions are that pair's side condition,
the compatibility of \texttt{c} with the null point, which the guard makes hold rather than
demand.

\rulelink{rule:null}{\texttt{(null)}} above makes \texttt{union(c,\ null)} a term, so both spellings exist, and the relation between
them is a \textbf{dichotomy}, not a trade-off. It is the wrapper's definition:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  where \texttt{c} does not touch the null point — \texttt{e\_c(null)} and \texttt{p\_c(⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩)} both undefined —
  \texttt{nullable(c)} and \texttt{union(c,\ null)} are the \textbf{same codec};
\item
  where \texttt{c} does touch it, \texttt{nullable(c)} is \textbf{equal to \texttt{c}}: the wrapper adds
  nothing. \texttt{nullable(opaque)\ =\ opaque}, \texttt{nullable(nullable(c))\ =\ nullable(c)}, which §6's
  idempotence node states as an equation, and \texttt{nullable(union(…,\ null))\ =\ union(…,\ null)}.
\end{itemize}

So \texttt{nullable} is either the union or the identity, and the precedence that distinguishes it buys
nothing a schema would want. (The two are also \texttt{≡} unconditionally; they
differ as codecs only in definedness, and only where the wrapper is redundant. The single term
outside both readings is \texttt{nullable(null)} — member \texttt{⊤} over the \texttt{NULL} point, of no use.)

Which settles what \texttt{nullable} is: \textbf{sugar} — idempotent insertion of the null option into a pair
that may already have it. It expresses nothing new, it costs no premise, and its idempotence is a
property of insertion rather than a fact about \texttt{𝟙}. The one term outside both branches of the
dichotomy is \texttt{nullable(null)}, which under the guard is simply \texttt{null}.

\texttt{union(c,\ null)} replaces it at any subterm. Where no discharger decides the pair, the pair
carries \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} as an \textbf{obligation}, not a refusal, so \texttt{union(c,\ null)} is
derivable there. The certificate reads a \texttt{held} through its two views, so a \texttt{held} subterm
beside \texttt{null} is certified where the views reach the bottom.

\section{§4 Fields are not codecs}\label{4-fields-are-not-codecs}

A second judgement, and the stratification is the point: presence is a property of a name binding,
never of an attribute.

\begin{verbatim}
⊢ f : T ⊗ π ⇝ μ    π ∈ {req, opt}     f holds T, with presence π, stored as μ
\end{verbatim}

A field carries a member like anything else — an optional attribute is still stored somewhere, and
an index key needs to know where. What a field carries that an attribute does not is
the \texttt{⊗\ π}, and that is the whole stratification: a rule demanding \texttt{⊢\ c\ :\ T\ ⇝\ μ} cannot be given
something whose judgement has a \texttt{⊗}.

Here \texttt{n\ :\^{}opt\ T} is a binding the record may omit, \texttt{n\ :\^{}req\ T} one it may not, and \texttt{R(ρ)} is the open

declared name is always held by its own set, so \texttt{⊕} is total and there is no collision to resolve.
The open part is
\texttt{∅} where \texttt{ρ} is absent, and \texttt{Name\ ⇀fin\ T} for a rest codec holding \texttt{T}. An object with no

\texttt{dom(e)} and \texttt{dom(p)}, and no item loses a name on the round trip. A caller who wants undeclared
names kept declares a rest codec.

\subsection{The stratification does work}\label{the-stratification-does-work}

No rule derives \texttt{⊢\ optional(c)\ :\ T\ ⇝\ μ}. Presence lives in \texttt{⊗\ π} and nowhere else, so \texttt{optional} is a field.
\texttt{array}'s premise is an attribute judgement. Nor \texttt{set}, \texttt{tuple}, \texttt{record}, nor a union option, for the
same reason: none of those positions has a name for anything to be absent from.

\section{§5 Choice, and its side conditions}\label{5-choice-and-its-side-conditions}

The rules a grammar cannot state, because they relate siblings rather than a term to its subterms:
the condition that makes a union lawful, and the decidable tests that discharge it.

\subsection{Compatibility}\label{compatibility}

A union's pair is the \textbf{join} of its options. Each option answers, per input and per side,
whether the input could be its: a value on the way out, an attribute on the way in. The union
acts through the first option that answers. Write \texttt{V(c)\ ⊆\ Value} for the values an option may be
asked to write and \texttt{A(c)\ ⊆\ 𝔸} for the attributes it may be asked to read.

\begin{definition}[(Compat) compatibility]
\label{cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.Compat, Calculus.PairwiseCompat}
\leanok
\begin{verbatim}
Compat(c, d)     ∀ a t.  p_c(a) = t  ∧  (a ∈ dom(p_d)  ∨  t ∈ dom(e_d))
                                     ⟹  p_d(a) = t
union(c₁ … cₙ)   obliges  ∀ i j.  Compat(cᵢ, cⱼ)
\end{verbatim}
Two options agree wherever they overlap. An attribute that both read, they read to one value. A
value that one option reads from an attribute, the other writes at that attribute or leaves alone.
The union obliges each pair in both orders, so the obligation is symmetric. It is the compatibility of inverse
semigroups: two partial bijections are compatible exactly when their union is a partial bijection
\cite{inverse-semigroups}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[a compatible union is lawful]
\label{thm:join-lawful}
\uses{cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.lawful_altCodec, Calculus.Alg.altCodec_e_of_mem}
\leanok
Over lawful, pairwise compatible options, the union is lawful, and it writes each value that any
option writes, at the attribute that option writes.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[the order of the options is unobservable]
\label{thm:join-perm}
\uses{cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.altCodec_perm}
\leanok
Over lawful, pairwise compatible options, a permutation of the options gives the same codec, on
the nose.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[without compatibility the order is observable]
\label{thm:join-order}
\lean{Calculus.flip_order_observable, Calculus.not_compat_flip}
\leanok
\texttt{boolean} beside its negation under \texttt{held} is two lawful options that are not compatible,
and the two orders write \texttt{true} at two different attributes.
\end{theorem}

So declaration order enters the definition of the union and leaves again as a theorem, and
compatibility is the condition under which it leaves. A value that two compatible options admit has
no owner, because no operation can observe one.

\textbf{The index is exact under the obligations.} A union writes wherever one of its options writes, so
a derivable term whose obligations hold holds exactly the values it admits (\texttt{typed\_T\_adm}), a union
included. A set is the rule that reads the obligations: its element writes injectively and at one member. An empty
option is the unit of the sum: it is compatible with every option (\texttt{pairwiseCompat\_never\_left},
\texttt{pairwiseCompat\_never\_right}).

\begin{definition}[(union)]
\label{rule:union}
\uses{cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.Typed.union}
\leanok
\begin{verbatim}
⊢ cᵢ : Tᵢ ⇝ μᵢ  (i ∈ 1…n)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── (union)
⊢ union(c₁ … cₙ) : ⋃ Tᵢ ⇝ (μ if ∀i. μᵢ = μ;  else ⊤)
\end{verbatim}
The rule carries no side condition. \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} is an obligation of the derivation
(\texttt{Obligations}), and soundness takes it as a premise.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[(dunion)]
\label{rule:dunion}
\uses{rule:object,rule:literal}
\begin{verbatim}
⊢ oᵢ = object(sᵢ, ρᵢ) : Tᵢ ⇝ M    sᵢ(d) = literal(tᵢ)    tᵢ pairwise distinct
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── (dunion)
⊢ discriminatedUnion(d, o₁ … oₙ) : ⋃ Tᵢ ⇝ M
\end{verbatim}
\end{definition}

Note that \rulelink{rule:dunion}{\texttt{(dunion)}}'s premise asks \texttt{sᵢ(d)} to \emph{be} the literal — an attribute term, hence a \texttt{req}
field. A row without the discriminator does not select a core option. A caller that accepts such
an old row supplies one outer \texttt{extendRead} reader for the whole union.

\textbf{Arity one at the discriminator}, and n-ary literals everywhere else. The rule asks for a single
tag \texttt{tᵢ} per variant, and disjointness of singletons is distinctness. Soundness does not need the
restriction: \rulelink{rule:literal}{\texttt{(literal)}} is n-ary, a set of tags is a perfectly good
admissible set, and disjoint options are compatible, so pairwise disjointness discharges the
obligation.

What required it is what a discriminated union \emph{is}. It is a \textbf{lookup}: the stored tag chooses
the variant. A variant answering to two tags is a key that does not identify its variant, and a
reader who has the tag still cannot say which shape they hold without consulting the table twice.

The join decides real schemas, and the next example is one.
\texttt{union(array(literal("A")),\ array(literal("B")))} has both options admitting \texttt{{[}{]}}, and both write
\texttt{⟨L,\ {[}{]}⟩}. So the options are compatible (\texttt{pairwiseCompat\_array\_literals}), and the union writes
the empty array and reads it back.

\subsection{The dischargers}\label{the-dischargers}

\rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} quantifies over inputs, so it is not decidable at the declaration, where a
codec is built and its subterms are known but no input is. The rest of this section is its
\textbf{dischargers}: decidable conditions, each sufficient for \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} on the
unions it covers:

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Discharger & Covers & From \\
\midrule
held-free options & every pair of terms with no \texttt{held} & both sit below \texttt{opaque}, which reads each value from one attribute (\texttt{pairwiseCompat\_of\_HF}) \\
a common canonical bound & two restrictions of one adapter & \texttt{compat\_of\_common\_bound}; the library's warrant is \texttt{pairwiseCompat\_of\_sub}, where its rule \texttt{below(x,\ y)} shows one option below the other: a literal below its base, a union whose options are each below, the same adapter over sides that are each below, or a term with no payload below itself \\
separation & two options that no input reaches both of & the certificate below (\texttt{pairwiseCompat\_of\_separated}) \\
an empty option & a pair with \texttt{never} on a side & \texttt{pairwiseCompat\_never\_left}, \texttt{pairwiseCompat\_never\_right} \\
kind exclusivity & \texttt{opaque}'s fixpoint (§3) & \texttt{compat\_opaque} \\
tag disjointness & \rulelink{rule:dunion}{\texttt{(dunion)}}, outright & the discriminator literals \\
a slice & a sublist of a compatible union & \texttt{pairwiseCompat\_sublist}, \texttt{pairwiseCompat\_extract}, \texttt{pairwiseCompat\_exclude} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textbf{Separation is one condition at a different projection.} Fix a partial map from inputs to
labels, such as a stored member, a runtime kind or a discriminator's tag, and give each option the set of
labels it answers to. Where those sets are pairwise disjoint, no input can reach two options,
because the label is a property of the \emph{input} and lands in at most one set. That is the whole
argument.

Said more directly, without projections at all: \textbf{each option gets one set of values it may write
and one set of attributes it may read, and separation is disjointness of those two families.} A
projection is a way of \emph{building} such a set — a runtime shape gives the values whose shape it
allows, \texttt{μ} the attributes whose member it allows, a discriminator's tag the items carrying one of
its tags. \textbf{Option admission} is the family itself, the finest there is: the set the union
consults when it asks whether an option could take the input.

Read that way the admission row stops offering a \emph{choice} between a shape and a set. The two constraints
simply \textbf{intersect} — an option may write \texttt{v} only if its shape allows the constructor of \texttt{v} \emph{and}
it admits \texttt{v} — and disjointness of the intersections follows from disjointness in either factor. A
coarse shape and an exact set are two factors of one family rather than two mechanisms, which is why
one row carries both. The two families are also independent: the writable side may be separated by
a shape while the readable side is separated by a member, and nothing has to reconcile the two.

What is left to distinguish the rows is one thing only: whether a row constrains \emph{both} families or
one. \texttt{μ} labels attributes and nothing else, so the member row settles the readable side and never
the writable one. A discriminator's tag and §1's kind label both, which is why those two settle a
pair outright. A pair is separated when both sides are separated, by whatever combination of rows
reaches them.

Read that way, \rulelink{rule:dunion}{\texttt{(dunion)}} is not a second kind of sum. It is \rulelink{rule:union}{\texttt{(union)}} with admission \textbf{read
off} the discriminator instead of attempted, and its separation needs no discharge, because reading a
label off an input reaches one variant by construction.

What no discharger covers is an undischarged obligation. The model still derives the union, and
soundness does not reach it. The library refuses it at the declaration, because the pair is not
shown compatible.

\begin{definition}[the certificate]
\label{def:certificate}
\uses{cond:compat,def:family}
\lean{Calculus.certPair, Calculus.meet', Calculus.meetNever', Calculus.viewsNever}
\leanok
Two options are certified where their meet is the bottom. A term denotes a partial isomorphism, and its caller view and its stored
view are its domain and its range. Two terms whose domains and ranges meet at the bottom are
orthogonal, and the meet of two orthogonal elements is the bottom (Lawson, \emph{Primer on inverse
semigroups I}, Lemma 2.16). So the meet answers the bottom there, and a \texttt{held} reaches the
meet through its views. On a held-free term the caller view is the term itself. The view of a union is
the union of its options' views, because a union admits where one of its options admits. The view
of a recursive term is the recursion of the view of its body, so a \texttt{held} below a recursion
reaches the meet too. The
meet refuses a closure beside a partner that its inner term does not meet at the bottom. A certified pair is separated
(\texttt{separates\_of\_certPair}), so it is compatible. The certificate is sufficient and not
necessary: two restrictions of one adapter that overlap are compatible, and no certificate reaches
them.
\end{definition}

\subsection{The join is monotone}\label{the-join-is-monotone}

First-match resolution grows with its options where the options that admit agree. It is stated as
first-match plus an obligation: the rule takes the first admitter and never compares attributes,
and the obligation says that the choice did not matter. Over lawful, compatible options the
obligation holds, because each option that admits a value writes it at one attribute
(\rulelink{thm:join-lawful}{the lawful-join Theorem}).

\begin{theorem}[first-match resolution is monotone]
\label{thm:confluent-mono}
\uses{cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.selectF_mono, Calculus.Alg.selectF_mono_list}
\leanok
Monotone, given that the options that admit agree.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[first-match resolution is continuous]
\label{thm:confluent-continuous}
\uses{cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.selectF_continuous_list, Calculus.Alg.selectF_continuous}
\leanok
Continuous, and in general this needs the obligation \textbf{at each stage} too. The two-option case
escapes it by putting the growing option first, which makes it automatically the first admitter.
\end{theorem}

These are the obligations of a least-fixpoint construction, and the model's recursion does not need
them. \rulelink{thm:guarded-recursion}{Guarded recursion} reads each input at the stage its own depth names, so it builds
no chain. The guard alone makes the stages agree past the depth of the input. \texttt{opaque} is that
construction at one body, and its options are kind-exclusive at every stage (\texttt{compat\_opaque}), so
\texttt{lawful\_opaq} proves its fixed point lawful.

\subsection{The machinery of admission}\label{the-machinery-of-admission}

The admission row asks, of each option and each side, which inputs the option may be given:
\texttt{V(c)} and \texttt{A(c)} above. Two things build them, both by structural recursion over the terms of §3–§4, and
both total.

\begin{theorem}[a term's admissible sets are exact]
\label{thm:admissible-exact}
\lean{Calculus.Adm_exact, Calculus.ExactV, Calculus.AdmA_exact, Calculus.ExactA}
\leanok
A codec is a pair, and an admissible set is a claim about one half of it at a time, so there are two
and not one: the values \texttt{e} accepts, and the attributes \texttt{p} accepts. Each is a \textbf{domain
rather than a description of one} — \texttt{dom(e)} and \texttt{dom(p)} — at every term of the calculus, with
no hypothesis and no term excepted. Nothing is over-approximated, and no term's answer is \texttt{⊤}.
\end{theorem}

That is available only because a row may \textbf{ask the codec} rather than describe it, and the three
terms force the point:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{refine(c,\ q)}.} \texttt{q} is a predicate on values, so the writable side is \texttt{V(c)\ ∩\ q} directly. The
  readable side cannot follow syntactically, \texttt{q} testing the value a read \emph{produces}, so it decodes
  with the base and applies \texttt{q} to the result.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{extendRead(c,\ r)}.} \texttt{p} reads attributes \texttt{c} does not, so a passthrough would claim a bound on
  exactly the direction \texttt{r} widens: \texttt{extendRead(literal("A"),\ r)} with \texttt{r} reading \texttt{⟨N,\ "1"⟩} to \texttt{"A"}
  reads an attribute \texttt{A(literal("A"))} refuses, which is unsound rather than coarse. The attributes
  \texttt{r} widens by are \texttt{dom(r)}, and \texttt{r} is data the outer carrier holds, so the set is \texttt{A(c)\ ∪\ dom(r)}. The
  writable side passes through: \texttt{extendRead} adds no way to write.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{held(c,\ f,\ g)}}, the one term whose readable side sees further than its writable one. \texttt{f} sits
  in front of \texttt{e}, so \texttt{V} pulls back along it; \texttt{g} sits behind \texttt{p} and is total, so it changes which
  value comes out and never which attributes go in, and \texttt{A} passes through.
\end{itemize}

What exactness costs is decidability. \texttt{held} carries an
arbitrary \texttt{f}, \texttt{refine} an arbitrary \texttt{q}, so membership of an admissible set is not a question the
declaration can answer. Admission \textbf{specifies} the two families. It does not decide them. What the
certificate runs is the meet, and the meet is decided. The bottom rows of the
meet at two formers read the shape classes of the universe, and the read-side proof of the DynamoDB
leaf meet uses occupancy.

\textbf{Admission is what a union consults, at every option.} A union attempts each option's codec, an
object option included, so \texttt{V(c)} and \texttt{A(c)} are \emph{option} admission as well: \texttt{dom(e)} and
\texttt{dom(p)} at every term. A closed object refuses an undeclared name on both halves, so an object option
admits a map exactly where its codec writes or reads the map.

One pair shows what the attempt decides. Take two objects declaring the same single \texttt{req} name and
different field terms, \texttt{⟨a\ :\ String⟩} against \texttt{⟨a\ :\ Number⟩}. Their admissible sets are disjoint on
both sides: a map the first writes carries a string at \texttt{a}, which the second's field term refuses. So
\texttt{⟨a\ ↦\ "1"⟩} reaches the first option alone. Their meet is the bottom, because the two field terms meet
at \texttt{never} at a required name, and the certificate accepts the pair on that meet.

An open object's rest takes part in the attempt: an undeclared name reaches the option where its rest
codec holds the name. An \texttt{opt} binding \textbf{widens} admission, which makes two options differing only
in optional names the overlap to watch: two objects declaring only optional names both admit the
empty map, however disjoint their names. Where both are held-free they are compatible, and the
union writes the empty map once. \rulelink{rule:dunion}{\texttt{(dunion)}}'s disjoint-literal premise is the
special case where the discriminator's names decide alone.

\begin{definition}[occupancy]
\label{def:occupancy}
\uses{cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.occV, Calculus.Alg.occA}
\leanok
One coarse label per term per side: which root shape of a value the term may write, and which root
shape of an attribute it may store. A universe supplies the shapes. At DynamoDB they are the
constructor of \texttt{Value} and the member of §1. The read-side proof of the DynamoDB leaf meet uses occupancy
(\texttt{AdmA\_and\_false}).
\end{definition}

Both over-approximate, and over-approximating is the safe direction — if \texttt{X\ ⊆\ X′}, \texttt{Y\ ⊆\ Y′} and
\texttt{X′\ ∩\ Y′\ =\ ∅} then \texttt{X\ ∩\ Y\ =\ ∅} — so a coarse answer costs a discharge and never buys a wrong one.
A \texttt{held} takes its write occupancy from its caller-side term and its read occupancy from its
stored-side term, so its two labels can differ. A \texttt{refine} takes its subterm's
occupancy. \texttt{mu\ b} takes the occupancy of its body, with a variable at no label. The proof is an induction over
the stages, and it needs no guard (\texttt{occV\_open}, \texttt{occA\_open}). Only \texttt{opaque} answers
\texttt{⊤}, every label.

\textbf{The writable side's label is the constructor of the value, not the runtime kind}, and the
difference does work. A kind collapses the three set families into one; the constructors do not, and
\texttt{set(string)} writes a set of strings where \texttt{set(number)} writes a set of numerals. Ten constructors
separate what eight kinds cannot. The readable side's label is the member, which the judgement's \texttt{μ}
index already computes.

\texttt{⊤} is \texttt{opaque}'s answer, because \texttt{opaque} accepts every kind by design. A \texttt{held} has a
label of its own: \texttt{flipBool} beside \texttt{null} is certified. That \texttt{opaque} accepts every kind is also what makes its
fixpoint's options pairwise kind-exclusive.

\begin{definition}[the family, conjoined]
\label{def:family}
\uses{def:occupancy,cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.OrthSV, Calculus.OrthSA}
\leanok
An option's set of values is its occupancy intersected with
its option admission, and its set of attributes likewise.
\end{definition}

Disjointness in either factor is enough: \textbf{on one side, two options separate
when their occupancies on that side are disjoint. On both sides at once, they separate when one
option requires a name the other does not declare.} The certificate asks the second factor, through
the meet: two closed bodies meet at the bottom where one requires a name that the other does not
declare. That is a question about two terms, answered by computation at the declaration. What it declines is an undischarged obligation, not a refusal of
compatibility.

Note where two container options overlap, and that it is a fact about the two terms rather than a
limit of any table: a list, a record and an object admit their empty case whatever their element
admits, so two such options share exactly one input: the empty array, or the empty map. Where the options are compatible,
both write that input at one attribute, and the union writes it. The same union built from
\texttt{object}s is separated outright, by names: a \texttt{req} binding is a name the empty item lacks, so
there is no empty case to share.

\texttt{union(array(string),\ array(number))} is not separated: both options write a sequence, both store
\texttt{L}, and both hold the empty array. It is compatible all the same, because both options are
held-free.

\texttt{union(set(string),\ set(number))} \emph{is} discharged, and on both sides, because
the writable side is the one that labels by constructor. Those options store \texttt{SS} and \texttt{NS}, so
reading was never in doubt; and they write a set of strings and a set of numerals, which are two
constructors, so the writing is settled as well. What remains is a loss of a different kind: a set's
member is computed from its element, so the options are \texttt{⇝\ SS} and \texttt{⇝\ NS} and the union's own \texttt{μ} is
\texttt{⊤} by the \rulelink{rule:union}{\texttt{(union)}} rule. A \texttt{⊤} term is one only \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} constrains, where a \texttt{⇝\ SS} term is pinned
by \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} and its read-side corollary. That loss is the union's and not the discharger's.

Two pairs show the two families failing independently, one each way. \texttt{union(string,\ decimal)} writes a string at both options — a decimal is \emph{held} as a string — and
stores \texttt{S} at one and \texttt{N} at the other, so the readable side separates and the writable one does
not. \texttt{union(number,\ decimal)} is the mirror: a number writes a numeral and a decimal a string, and
both store \texttt{N}, so the writable side separates and the readable one does not. Neither pair is
separated, and each fails on a different half, which is why the two families are asked separately and
never reconciled. Neither pair is compatible either: the first writes one value at two attributes,
and the second reads one attribute to two values.

\textbf{Attempting an option answers a real question.} Whether a term \emph{decides its own membership} —
whether "this option accepted the value" is evidence at all — is answered by an admissible set that
\emph{is} \texttt{dom(e)}, and nothing is left for a second condition to add. \texttt{union(literal("A"),\ literal("B"))} is separated
because \texttt{\{"A"\}} and \texttt{\{"B"\}} do not meet; \texttt{union(literal("A"),\ string)} is not, because \texttt{\{"A"\}} sits
inside every string. That pair is compatible all the same, because both options are held-free. A term that decides nothing about its values has the widest domain, and the
widest domain is the one that fails to be disjoint.

Two counterexamples bound the question, and each refutes one form of it:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{One side is not enough.} \texttt{union(literal("A"),\ string)} has the left option deciding its own
  membership, so "either option decides" is satisfied, and both options admit \texttt{"A"}. A real answer
  from one option does not separate a pair; the other option is still answering.
\item
  \textbf{Two sides are not enough either.} \texttt{union(array(literal("A")),\ array(literal("B")))} has both
  options as decisive as a term can be, and both admit \texttt{{[}{]}}. Deciding at the element does not
  decide at the container: every list, record and object admits its empty case, which no element
  ever sees. A set has none, because \texttt{℘⁺fin} has no empty inhabitant.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{A pair sharing a base separates where neither term's own sets do}. \texttt{number} and
every numeric literal are the \textbf{same base}: \texttt{WFNumeral}, which a literal refines at
\texttt{∈\ ℓ}. Two literals over disjoint
value sets are two refinements of one base; refinements of one base read an attribute to the \emph{same}
value, so at most one predicate accepts it, and both sides separate.
\texttt{refine(number,\ positive)} against \texttt{refine(number,\ negative)} is the same argument outside \texttt{literal}.
What carries it is the pair: \texttt{V} and \texttt{A} answer about one term at a time, and this answer is about
two.

\texttt{held} reaches a pair no other row does, and on one side only.
\texttt{union(literal("A"),\ held(literal("B"),\ f,\ g))} separates on the readable side for \emph{every} \texttt{f} and
\texttt{g}, the two sets being \texttt{\{⟨S,\ "A"⟩\}} and \texttt{\{⟨S,\ "B"⟩\}}: the wrapper never decides, the subterm does,
and reading through the wrapper is what lets the question be asked of it. The writable side stays an
obligation, \texttt{f} being free to move a value into any constructor. And
\texttt{union(literal("A"),\ held(literal("A"),\ f,\ g))}, the counterexample below, separates on neither
side. The member projection cannot distinguish either pair: both options are \texttt{⇝\ S}.

One note on what has no set of its own. \texttt{optional} is a \textbf{field} (§4), not a term,
so nothing passes through it: admission is a function on terms and has no case to give it. And a
union's own admission is the join of its options': the set where one option admits
(\texttt{Adm\_union}).

Two open object options \texttt{⟨a\ :\ String⟩} and \texttt{⟨b\ :\ String⟩} share the inhabitant
\texttt{⟨a\ ↦\ "1",\ b\ ↦\ "2"⟩}, so they are not separated. They are compatible, because both are
held-free, and the union writes the shared value once.

Disjoint held sets do not discharge compatibility, and the reason is the same confusion twice:
\textbf{held sets are sets of values, and compatibility's readable side quantifies over attributes.}
\texttt{held} is precisely the term that separates the two, because its adapter relabels, so two options can
hold disjoint sets of values and still read one attribute, each to its own value.
\texttt{union(literal("A"),\ held(literal("A"),\ f,\ g))} with \texttt{f} and \texttt{g} swapping \texttt{"B"} for \texttt{"A"} holds
\texttt{\{"A"\}} and \texttt{\{"B"\}}, which are disjoint on the nose, and both options read \texttt{S\ "A"} — the first to
\texttt{"A"}, the second to \texttt{"B"}. Such a pair is not compatible (\texttt{not\_compat\_shout} is the witness,
with \texttt{"X"} and \texttt{"x"}).

So no condition on held sets discharges compatibility. What decides the readable side is a condition
on \emph{stored attributes}, distinct members \texttt{μᵢ\ ≠\ μⱼ} or disjoint readable-side admission, which
the separation row takes. Attributes rather than values is the point: a condition on values cannot
decide a question about attributes.

\subsection{Soundness of the judgement}\label{theorem-soundness}

\begin{theorem}[soundness]
\label{thm:soundness}
\uses{cond:R,cond:C,cond:W,cond:compat,rule:union,rule:dunion,rule:opaque,rule:mu,thm:guarded-recursion}
\lean{Calculus.soundness, Calculus.Alg.soundnessIn, Calculus.Alg.carrier_denote}
\leanok
Every derivable \texttt{⊢\ c\ :\ T\ ⇝\ μ}, and every \texttt{⊢\ f\ :\ T\ ⊗\ π\ ⇝\ μ}, whose obligations hold, denotes a pair
satisfying \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}, \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} and \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}}. The obligations are each
\rulelink{rule:held}{\texttt{(held)}}'s iso premise, each \rulelink{rule:union}{\texttt{(union)}}'s \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}}, and each
\rulelink{rule:set}{\texttt{(set)}}'s set obligation (\texttt{SetObl}): the rule of the universe on a set element, which is
\texttt{SetElem} at DynamoDB. Soundness takes the set obligation as a premise and does not read it. The
dischargers of this section settle \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} at the declaration wherever they decide
a pair, \rulelink{rule:dunion}{\texttt{(dunion)}} and \texttt{opaque} outright. A \rulelink{rule:mu}{\texttt{(mu)}} owes the obligations of its
body at each stage, given that the stage before is lawful. The induction runs over open terms, with
an environment of lawful codecs for the variables. The closed theorem is its case at the empty
environment. The host's recursive schema for JSON is derived and lawful through it (\texttt{lawful\_json}).
The theorem holds at every universe signature. A universe supplies the laws of its primitives, of its
formers and of its top, and the generic combinators supply the rest.
\end{theorem}

The two premises on \rulelink{rule:legacy}{\texttt{(alternate reader)}}, \texttt{ran(r)\ ⊆\ dom(e)} and
coherence, exist because this proof failed without them. Each excludes a term that the calculus
would otherwise derive and the laws refuse. Null-honesty is not
among them: the null point is an option of \rulelink{rule:union}{\texttt{(union)}}, so what would have been a
premise is that sum's \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}}, and the sugar paragraph of §3 settles it.

\begin{lemma}[injectivity]
\label{lem:injectivity}
\uses{cond:R}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.Lawful.e_injective}
\leanok
\rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} makes \texttt{e} injective on \texttt{dom(e)}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\uses{cond:R}
\leanok
If \texttt{e(t)\ =\ e(t′)} then \texttt{t\ =\ p(e(t))\ =\ p(e(t′))\ =\ t′}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[traversal]
\label{lem:traversal}
\uses{cond:R,cond:C}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.entrywise, Calculus.Alg.lawful_entrywise, Calculus.Alg.carrier_entrywise}
\leanok
One proof shape serves every product. Let a \emph{step} be an entrywise pair
between the entries of two lists, and lift it entrywise — defined where every entry is, positions
preserved. If the step satisfies a law pointwise, the lift satisfies it listwise.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\uses{cond:R,cond:C}
\leanok
\rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} because
positions re-read in place, \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} because each entry re-encodes at the attribute it
was read from, and lengths survive both directions. Keyed products need no extra clause: \texttt{M} is
a name set with a function on it, so keys are unique by type and there is no first occurrence to
prefer. Proved once; every product case
below is an instantiation.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
\leanok
By induction on the derivation, with one refinement at \rulelink{rule:mu}{\texttt{(mu)}}. The induction runs over open
terms, with an environment of lawful codecs for the variables (\texttt{soundnessIn}). Each case
first says what the pair \emph{is} — the denotation is part of the rule — then discharges the laws. The
grouping is the algebra of §7: points, wrappers, products, sums.

\textbf{Points.} Each ground term writes one member, totally on its held set, and reads exactly that
member back into the held set. \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} and \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} are immediate, and
\rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} is the carrier's round trip: identity for \texttt{string}, \texttt{boolean}, \texttt{binary},
\texttt{null} (whose held set is the single value \texttt{null} and whose payload is \texttt{∗}), \texttt{number} and a numeric
\texttt{literal}, whose carrier is \texttt{WFNumeral} on both halves. \texttt{decimal} is \texttt{held} over \texttt{number}, below: a
numeral in \texttt{Stored} is returned as written, which is what \texttt{Stored} is chosen to make true, so
\texttt{Can(decimal)} is the normalised part of \texttt{N}.

\textbf{Wrappers.}

\emph{held.} \texttt{e\ =\ e\_c\ ∘\ f}, \texttt{p\ =\ g\ ∘\ p\_c}. \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}: \texttt{g(p\_c(e\_c(f\ t)))\ =\ g(f\ t)\ =\ t}. \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}:
\texttt{e(p(a))\ =\ e\_c(f(g(p\_c\ a)))\ =\ e\_c(p\_c\ a)\ =\ a}, by the iso premise and the premise's
\rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}. \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}}: the member is untouched.

\emph{refine.} \texttt{e\ =\ e\_c} on \texttt{q}; \texttt{dom(p)\ =\ p\_c⁻¹(q)}. All three laws restrict.

\emph{legacy.} \texttt{e\ =\ e\_c}; \texttt{p\ =\ p\_c\ ∪\ r}, a function \emph{because} of coherence — without that premise the
definition needs a precedence between the two readings, and a precedence between two incompatible
readings is a union whose order is observable, which \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} exists to refuse. \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}: \texttt{e\_c(t)\ ∈\ dom(p\_c)}
by the premise's \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}, and coherence makes any overlap with \texttt{dom(r)} agree, so the composite
reads it to \texttt{t}. The weaker form of \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}: on \texttt{dom(p\_c)} by the premise; on the
extension it \emph{is} \texttt{ran(r)\ ⊆\ dom(e)}: without it a migrated attribute reads and the write-back
is the failure. \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}}: unchanged. \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} itself: on \texttt{dom(p\_c)} by
hypothesis; on the extension it is what the reader is licensed to break, and no further: \texttt{e(r(a))} is canonical, so the breach does not survive its
own round trip, which is the precise sense in which a migrated row behaves like a current one from
the first rewrite on.

\emph{nullable.} Two cases, by the guard of §3, and neither carries a premise.

Where \texttt{owns(c)}, the pair \emph{is} \texttt{c}'s, \texttt{e\ =\ e\_c} and \texttt{p\ =\ p\_c}, and all three laws are the
hypothesis, restated at a \texttt{⊤} index where \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}} is vacuous.

Where it does not, \texttt{e}: \texttt{null\ ↦\ ⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩}, else \texttt{e\_c}; \texttt{p}: \texttt{⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩\ ↦\ null}, else \texttt{p\_c}, and
what were the honesty premises are now available as facts. \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} at \texttt{null} is immediate; at
\texttt{t\ ≠\ null}, \texttt{e\_c(t)\ ≠\ ⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩}, because if it were, the hypothesis's \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}} would give
\texttt{p\_c(⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩)\ =\ t} and \texttt{owns(c)} would hold. So the \texttt{NULL} branch cannot shadow the reading —
without the guard, \texttt{nullable(held(nullable(string),\ f,\ g))} with \texttt{f} moving a sentinel onto \texttt{null}
would encode the sentinel as \texttt{⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩} and read back \texttt{null}; with it, the inner \texttt{nullable} owns
the point and the outer one adds nothing. \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}: \texttt{⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩} round-trips; else \texttt{p\_c(a)\ ≠\ null},
because if it were, the hypothesis's \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} would put \texttt{null} in \texttt{dom(e\_c)} and \texttt{owns(c)} would
hold, so the \texttt{e\_c} branch answers, and it writes the attribute back by the hypothesis's
\rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}. What rules out a non-\texttt{NULL} attribute reading to \texttt{null} and re-encoding as
\texttt{⟨NULL,\ ∗⟩}, the member change that \rulelink{cond:MP}{\textbf{(MP)}} refuses, is the same fact.

\texttt{owns(nullable(c))} always, so the second case never nests: §6's idempotence is the construction,
not an equation the proof has to earn.

\emph{mu.} The model has no thunk. A thunk that re-enters its own schema is a \rulelink{rule:mu}{\texttt{(mu)}} with a
variable at the thunk, and any other thunk is the term it returns. What the re-entry passes through decides the
case.

\textbf{Through a container.} The guard accepts the binder, and \rulelink{thm:guarded-recursion}{guarded recursion} gives the
case. The stages are lawful by the induction over open terms, at an environment that holds the stage
before. The limit is lawful. Containers strictly shrink the input, which is what makes the stages
agree past the depth of the input.

\textbf{Through wrappers, or a union, alone.} The variable reads the binder's own input, the stages never
grow, and the guard refuses the binder, so no judgement derives the term. \textbf{Through a \texttt{held}.} The
conversion can make the input deeper, and the guard refuses the binder too.

\textbf{Fields.} A field denotes a pair over presence-extended domains — \texttt{e♭\ :\ T\ ⇀\ 𝔸\ ∪\ \{ε\}},
\texttt{p♭\ :\ (𝔸\ ∪\ \{ε\})\ ⇀\ T}, \texttt{ε} the absent binding — with \texttt{member(ε)} undefined and the laws read over
\texttt{𝔸} as before. \rulelink{rule:field}{\texttt{(field)}} and \rulelink{rule:optional}{\texttt{(optional)}} set \texttt{e♭\ =\ e}, \texttt{p♭\ =\ p}; absence is handled at \rulelink{rule:object}{\texttt{(object)}},
where an \texttt{opt} name absent in the item is a binding absent in the record. A required name
must be present on both current-schema sides.
\rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} quantifies over \texttt{𝔸}, so \texttt{ε} asks nothing.

\textbf{Products} — each an instantiation of the \rulelink{lem:traversal}{traversal Lemma}.

\emph{array, tuple, tuple-tail, record.} The step is the subterm's pair at each position or name — a
tuple first aligns positions with their codecs, head positionally and tail uniformly, length
mismatches refusing. The Lemma gives all three laws from the induction hypotheses; an \texttt{extendRead}
element is where the breach of \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} propagates, pointwise, on the positions it rereads,
per §2.

\emph{object.} The step dispatches each entry by its name: a declared name through its field, with the
\texttt{ε}-handling of the Fields case; an undeclared name by \texttt{ρ}, and a closed object refuses it. The presence guards — every \texttt{req} name demanded in
both directions, every non-\texttt{opt} binding demanded of a value — are what keep the step total where
item adds none. \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}}: \texttt{M}. \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}: the Lemma's keyed clause. A rest
policy that drops undeclared names is not lawful (\texttt{not\_lawful\_dropAll}), and no term has one.

\emph{set.} The premise is \texttt{Lawful\ c} and nothing else. The step writes each element through \texttt{e\_c} and
carries the payloads into the first wire family, \texttt{SS}, \texttt{NS} or \texttt{BS}, into which every element writes; it
fails where no family holds every element or where two elements share a payload. \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}: \texttt{e\_c} is
injective by the \rulelink{lem:injectivity}{injectivity Lemma}, so the written set holds one payload per element and the \rulelink{lem:traversal}{traversal Lemma} reads
each back; non-emptiness survives both directions. \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}: entrywise, each element is
written back at the attribute it was read from, so the same payload projection re-succeeds. \texttt{p} is injective on payloads as well: by \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} the element writes each read value back at the payload it was read from, so no two payloads read to one value, and the read set holds one value per payload. \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}}: \texttt{setMember\ μ} where the element names \texttt{μ}, and nothing where it names none. A set
has no interior members: the container fixes its elements' member, which is why the element's own
\rulelink{cond:MP}{\textbf{(MP)}} is never even invoked inside one.

\textbf{Sums.} Admission, per side: option \texttt{i} admits attribute \texttt{a} when \texttt{pᵢ(a)} is defined, and admits
value \texttt{t} when \texttt{eᵢ(t)} is defined. \texttt{e(t)\ =\ eᵢ(t)} for the first admitting \texttt{i}, else undefined;
\texttt{p(a)} dually. The laws, and where each needs \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}}:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}: \texttt{t\ ∈\ dom(e)} names the first admitting \texttt{i}, and \texttt{i} reads \texttt{eᵢ(t)} back (the
  premise's \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}). The first option \texttt{j} that admits \texttt{eᵢ(t)} acts at that attribute, so
  \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} makes \texttt{j} read it to \texttt{t}, and \texttt{p(e(t))\ =\ t}.
\item
  \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}: \texttt{a\ ∈\ dom(p)} names the first admitting \texttt{i}, and \texttt{eᵢ(pᵢ(a))\ =\ a} by the premise's
  \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}}. The first option \texttt{j} that admits \texttt{pᵢ(a)} acts at that value, so
  \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} makes \texttt{j} read \texttt{a} to \texttt{pᵢ(a)}, \texttt{j}'s own \rulelink{cond:C}{\textbf{(C)}} makes \texttt{j}
  write \texttt{pᵢ(a)} at \texttt{a}, and \texttt{e(p(a))\ =\ a}.
\item
  \rulelink{cond:W}{\textbf{(W)}}: when every \texttt{μᵢ\ =\ μ}, whichever option answers writes \texttt{⟨μ,\ \_⟩}.
\end{itemize}

The dischargers close the obligation where they apply:

\emph{dunion.} A canonical item of option \texttt{i} carries the discriminator with a tag in \texttt{Lᵢ}; option
\texttt{j\ ≠\ i} either does not admit the name set or its literal refuses the tag, \texttt{Lⱼ} being disjoint from
\texttt{Lᵢ}. So no input reaches two options, the options are separated, and the sum case closes. The one
union that is separated by construction, which is what the term is \emph{for}.

\emph{opaque.} The fixpoint of §3: one option per kind, each self-identifying by construction — kinds
partition the values and members partition the attributes. So its options are compatible at
every depth of the fixpoint (\texttt{compat\_opaque}), and the sum case closes here too.
\end{proof}

What remains outside the theorem is what §3 already names: each \rulelink{rule:union}{\texttt{(union)}}'s \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} where no discharger decides it. The numeral bound is not among
them: it is the carrier \texttt{WFNumeral} of \rulelink{rule:number}{\texttt{(number)}}, and a numeric \texttt{literal}'s base \emph{is}
\texttt{number}, so it is inside the theorem.

\section{§6 Equations}\label{6-equations}

Which rewrites preserve the pair. Read \texttt{≡} as: the same \texttt{T}, the same \texttt{μ}, and \texttt{e} and \texttt{p} agreeing
everywhere both are defined.

\subsection{Hold}\label{hold}

\begin{theorem}[held(c, id, id) ≡ c]
\label{eq:held-id}
\uses{rule:held}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.equiv_through_id, Calculus.equiv_denote_held_id}
\leanok
\texttt{held(c,\ id,\ id)\ ≡\ c}. Identity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[isos compose]
\label{eq:held-comp}
\uses{rule:held}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.equiv_through_comp, Calculus.equiv_denote_held_comp}
\leanok
\texttt{held(held(c,\ f,\ g),\ f′,\ g′)\ ≡\ held(c,\ f\ ∘\ f′,\ g′\ ∘\ g)}. Isos compose; note the
reversal on the reading half.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[prisms meet]
\label{eq:refine-meet}
\uses{rule:refine}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.equiv_restrict_meet, Calculus.equiv_denote_refine_meet}
\leanok
\texttt{refine(refine(c,\ q),\ q′)\ ≡\ refine(c,\ q\ ∩\ q′)}. Prisms meet.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[a full refinement is none]
\label{eq:refine-full}
\uses{rule:refine}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.equiv_restrict_full, Calculus.equiv_denote_refine_full}
\leanok
\texttt{refine(c,\ T)\ ≡\ c} for \texttt{⊢\ c\ :\ T\ ⇝\ μ}. A refinement admitting everything is no
refinement.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[a legacy reading that is the current one]
\label{eq:legacy-self}
\uses{rule:legacy}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.equiv_extendRead_self}
\leanok
\texttt{extendRead(c,\ p\_c)\ ≡\ c}. An older reading that is the current one adds nothing.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[a migration agrees on the canonical set]
\label{eq:legacy-can}
\uses{rule:legacy,cond:R}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.extendRead_eq_on_can}
\leanok
\texttt{extendRead(c,\ r)\ ≈\ c} on \texttt{Can(c)}, for lawful \texttt{c}. A migration differs from a
lawful current encoding only off the canonical set, and the premise it needs is \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[choice of one]
\label{eq:union-single}
\uses{rule:union}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.equiv_altCodec_single}
\leanok
\texttt{union(c)\ ≡\ c}. Choice of one.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[union is associative]
\label{eq:union-assoc}
\uses{rule:union}
\lean{Calculus.equiv_altCodec_assoc}
\leanok
\texttt{union(c₁,\ union(c₂,\ c₃))\ ≡\ union(c₁,\ c₂,\ c₃)}. Associative, with no side condition: the first
admitter of the nested union is the first admitter of the flat one, so nesting is flattening.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[union is commutative]
\label{eq:union-perm}
\uses{rule:union,cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.Alg.equiv_altCodec_perm, Calculus.Alg.altCodec_perm, Calculus.Alg.equiv_altCodec_swap}
\leanok
\texttt{union(c₁\ …\ cₙ)\ ≡\ union(c\_σ(1)\ …\ c\_σ(n))} for any permutation \texttt{σ}, over lawful, pairwise
compatible options. Commutative: the first-admitter rule reads declaration order, and
\rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} makes the order unobservable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[wrappers preserve the member]
\label{eq:wrapper-mu}
\leanok
preserve the member: a field's member is its attribute's (§4).
\end{theorem}

So wrapping a term never changes the member a key stores.

\subsection{Do not hold}\label{do-not-hold}

\begin{theorem}[a union of arrays is not an array of unions]
\label{eq:union-array}
\uses{rule:union,rule:array}
\lean{Calculus.not_equiv_union_array}
\leanok
\texttt{union(array(c₁),\ array(c₂))\ ≢\ array(union(c₁,\ c₂))}. Left: a sequence homogeneous in one
option. Right: a sequence of either, elementwise. Different held sets.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[a tuple with a tail is neither a tuple nor an array]
\label{eq:tuple-tail-neither}
\uses{rule:tuple-tail,rule:tuple,rule:array}
\lean{Calculus.not_equiv_tupleTail_tuple, Calculus.not_equiv_tupleTail_array}
\leanok
\texttt{tuple(c₁\ …\ cₙ,\ tail(c))\ ≢\ tuple(c₁\ …\ cₙ)\ } and \texttt{≢\ array(union(…))}. A fixed head
with a repeating tail is neither: a tuple has no tail and an array has no head.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[a refinement does not commute with an iso]
\label{eq:refine-held}
\uses{rule:refine,rule:held}
\lean{Calculus.not_equiv_refine_held}
\leanok
\texttt{refine(held(c,\ f,\ g),\ q)\ ≢\ held(refine(c,\ q),\ f,\ g)}. The refinement applies on opposite
sides of the iso, so \texttt{q} is a subset of different sets. Two different terms, both meaningful.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[a union is not closed under extension]
\label{eq:union-extend}
\uses{rule:union,cond:compat}
\lean{Calculus.not_equiv_union_extend}
\leanok
\texttt{union(c₁\ …\ cₙ)\ ≢\ union(c₁\ …\ cₙ,\ c)}. Not closed under extension: a further option grows the
held set, and it can also break the compatibility of the options.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[legacy breaks (MP), on purpose]
\label{eq:legacy-mp}
\uses{rule:legacy,cond:MP,cor:readW}
\lean{Calculus.legacy_breaks_MP}
\leanok
\texttt{extendRead(c,\ r)} can break \rulelink{cond:MP}{\textbf{(MP)}}. It is outside the term
judgement. Changing what is stored is the migration, and §2's read-side corollary falls with it.
\end{theorem}

So an adapted codec cannot be an index key, which is the price.

\section{§7 The three layers, and the algebra}\label{7-the-three-layers-and-the-algebra}

Three layers answer different questions, and conflating them is what makes a side condition look
optional. A grammar over the terms — a regular tree grammar, with nonterminals indexed by \texttt{μ} — says
which terms can be \emph{written}. It cannot express \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}}, which relates siblings rather
than a term to its subterms, and which is not about the term at all but about the inputs it is
given, so lawfulness needs the obligations of the union rather than a production.
And the judgement in turn says nothing about what a term \emph{means}; that is §2, the pair and its laws —
a connection §5's soundness theorem proves rather than leaves implied.

Which places the calculus: \textbf{§1} a universal domain, \textbf{§2} types as quotient retracts of it,
\textbf{§3–§5} a two-index typing relation with side conditions, tied back to §2 by soundness, \textbf{§6} the
equational theory. And the terms are generated by a small algebra, which the soundness proof's
grouping makes official: \textbf{points} (the ground terms); one \textbf{partial iso} (\texttt{held}, with \texttt{refine}
its prism); three \textbf{products} — positional (\texttt{array},
\texttt{tuple}) and keyed (\texttt{record}, \texttt{object}), instances of one traversal argument, and unordered (\texttt{set}),
which is the image argument, with injectivity from \rulelink{cond:R}{\textbf{(R)}};
one \textbf{sum} (\texttt{union}), the join of compatible options, with \rulelink{rule:dunion}{\texttt{(dunion)}} the one separated
by construction and \texttt{opaque} the one whose compatibility is a theorem; and a \textbf{fixpoint} (\texttt{mu},
and \texttt{opaque} as its instance). Fields are the keyed product's
presence decoration, and \texttt{nullable} is that sum's guarded insertion (§3) rather than a term of its own.
The outer read extension \texttt{extendRead} adds one agreeing read-extension to a complete current term.

The algebra makes one more thing visible, which the rules do not show. \textbf{The two halves of the pair are the same construction at two carriers, everywhere the
calculus builds something itself.} A sum filters by the admission \emph{that side} reads and acts
through the first option that answers: the same sentence for \texttt{e} and for \texttt{p}, with value-admission
swapped for attribute-admission. Adjoining the null point is the same construction on values and on
attributes. A product traverses. Reading a discriminator's tag is one lookup, spelled once per
carrier.

Exactly two term constructors break it: \texttt{held} and \texttt{refine}. Each
constructor carries a \textbf{caller's function that is not itself a codec}. \texttt{held}'s \texttt{f}
gates the writing while \texttt{g} only post-processes the reading, which is why the readable side sees
through it and the writable one cannot (§5). \texttt{refine}'s
\texttt{q} is a predicate on \emph{values}, so it gates one carrier directly and the other only after a read.
Outside the term algebra, \texttt{extendRead}'s \texttt{r} widens the reading and
touches the writing not at all.

So the asymmetry in this calculus is not a property of encoding-versus-decoding. It is the mark of
where a caller's function enters. It stays local to two term constructors and one outer carrier.
That is the divisible /
decidable \cite{divisible-decidable} skeleton, read invariantly — as Invariant rather than as a
contravariant functor — because both directions are present at once.

The judgement settles the laws for every term the calculus builds itself. What it leaves to the
caller is the premise on a function that is not a codec: \rulelink{rule:held}{\texttt{(held)}}'s inverse pair
and \rulelink{rule:refine}{\texttt{(refine)}}'s predicate. The outer
\rulelink{rule:legacy}{\texttt{(alternate reader)}} rule separately requires agreement with the current
reading. What no derivation settles is each
\texttt{union}'s \rulelink{cond:compat}{\textbf{(Compat)}} where no discharger decides it, and how much of any of this an
implementation carries in its own types, which is a question about that implementation and not
about the calculus.
